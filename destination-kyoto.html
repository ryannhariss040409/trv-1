Kyoto, Japan

Kyoto rewards routing more than any city we cover. Group temples by neighborhood, start earlier than feels reasonable, and the same trip goes from exhausting to unhurried.

Plan a Kyoto trip See the 6-day itinerary

Overview

Kyoto has around 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines. You will not see them all, and the visitors who enjoy the city most stop trying by day two. The useful mental model is that Kyoto is five distinct districts arranged in a rough ring, and a good day picks one district and walks it.

The districts that matter for a first visit are Higashiyama in the east, which holds Kiyomizu-dera and Gion; Arashiyama in the west, with the bamboo grove and the monkey park; the Fushimi area in the south, with the torii gates; central Kyoto around Nijo Castle and the Nishiki market; and northern Kyoto for Kinkaku-ji and the quieter Zen gardens. Crossing between two of these mid-day costs you 45 to 70 minutes on a bus. Doing it twice in one day is how people end up describing Kyoto as tiring.

Kyoto also carries real overtourism pressure. Gion has restricted photography on certain private lanes and residents have asked visitors to stay off them. Treat posted signs as rules rather than suggestions. Buses in Higashiyama during cherry blossom season can be standing-room only for forty minutes. None of this makes the city less worth visiting, but it does mean the schedule matters.

Kyoto is cheaper day-to-day than most US travelers expect. Temple admission is typically $3 to $6. A bowl of excellent ramen is $8 to $11. A convenience store breakfast that is genuinely good costs under $5. The expensive parts are the flight and, if you want it, one high-end kaiseki dinner, which can run $120 to $250 per person on its own.

On the rail pass question. The nationwide JR Pass rarely pays off for a trip that stays in the Kansai region. It becomes worth it when you are doing Tokyo to Kyoto to Hiroshima and back inside a week. For Kyoto plus Osaka plus Nara, buy an IC card, load it, and pay per ride. Run the arithmetic on your specific route before buying anything.

Getting around

Do not rent a car. Kyoto parking is expensive and scarce, the streets are narrow, and everything in this guide is reachable by train, bus or on foot.

Get an IC card on arrival. ICOCA, Suica and Pasmo all work interchangeably across Kyoto and Osaka. Tap in, tap out, no fare math. They also work in convenience stores and many vending machines.

Buses cover what trains miss, particularly northern and eastern Kyoto. They are slow in traffic and unpleasant in peak season. If a route offers a train alternative, take the train.

Walking is the real transport. Our three-day plan averages 6.5 miles per day on foot. Bring shoes you have already broken in, and remember that many temples require removing them.

Taxis are clean, metered and honest, and worth it for a tired evening. Expect $9 to $18 for a cross-district ride.

Where to stay

Kyoto Station area. Best for day trips to Nara, Osaka and Himeji. Least atmospheric, most convenient. Mid-range doubles $110 to $190.

Karasuma and Shijo, central. Our usual recommendation for a first visit. Walkable to Nishiki and Pontocho, on the subway, still quiet at night. $130 to $220.

Gion and Higashiyama. The most atmospheric and the most expensive. Traditional ryokan stays here run $280 to $600 per night, and one night is often the right amount.

Arashiyama. Beautiful in the evening after the day visitors leave, but a long ride from everything else. Good for a second visit, not a first.

Ryokan etiquette matters: shoes off at the entrance, futons laid out during dinner, and dinner served at a fixed time you agree to in advance. Book at least one if the budget allows.

Safety notes

Japan has one of the lowest violent crime rates in the world and Kyoto is safe to walk at night in essentially every district a visitor would be in. The practical risks are heat, etiquette missteps and natural events, not crime.

  • Summer heat is a genuine hazard. July and August routinely hit 95°F with high humidity. Carry water, use the convenience store air conditioning, and move temple visits to before 10:00 AM.
  • Respect photography restrictions in Gion. Certain private lanes carry fines for photography. Never photograph a geiko or maiko without asking, and never follow one down the street.
  • Cash still matters. Card acceptance has improved a lot, but small restaurants, temple admissions and some buses remain cash only. 7-Eleven ATMs reliably accept US cards.
  • Earthquakes are possible. Kyoto is less exposed than Tokyo but not immune. Learn where your accommodation's exit is, and if a quake happens, stay inside and away from windows.
  • Typhoon season runs roughly 08/01 to 10/15. A direct hit shuts down trains for a day. Build one flex day into any September trip.
  • Emergency numbers: 110 for police, 119 for fire and ambulance.

US citizens can enter Japan visa-free for tourism up to 90 days. Japan requires online arrival registration through its immigration system, which changes periodically, so check the official Japanese immigration site and the US State Department Japan page within a month of departure.

One thing worth knowing: some prescription medications that are routine in the US, including certain stimulants and codeine-containing cold medicine, are restricted or prohibited in Japan. Check before you pack. See our Disclaimer.
