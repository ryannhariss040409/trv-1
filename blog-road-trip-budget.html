Budgets

What a 2,000-mile road trip really costs

Published 12/11/2025 · Updated 06/30/2026 · 12 minute read · By Tobias Marchetti

Almost everyone underestimates a road trip, and they underestimate it in the same direction. Fuel gets calculated carefully, because fuel is the thing that feels like the cost of driving. Everything else gets waved at. Then the trip ends about 30 percent over what anyone expected and the explanation is always vague.

It is not vague. The overage is nearly always four specific line items, and they are the four listed at the end of this piece. First, the arithmetic that people do get right, done properly.

The trip we are pricing

Two adults, fourteen days, roughly 2,000 miles. A mid-size SUV rental, a mix of park camping and motels, and a route through the western US with several national parks. This is close to the median road trip our readers plan, and every number below scales linearly, so you can adjust for your own mileage.

Fuel: less than you think

The math is three numbers: miles, miles per gallon, and price per gallon. Two thousand miles in a vehicle that returns 26 miles per gallon uses about 77 gallons. At a national average around $3.30 per gallon that is roughly $254 for the entire trip.

Two adjustments matter. Gas is meaningfully more expensive in California, Washington and Hawaii, sometimes by a dollar or more per gallon, and cheaper across most of the interior. And loaded roof boxes, mountain grades and air conditioning at 100°F all reduce real-world efficiency, often by 10 to 15 percent. Budget $290 rather than $254 and you will be close.

The honest conclusion is that fuel is not the expensive part of a road trip. It is around 8 percent of the total. People fixate on it because it is the only cost that arrives in visible $60 chunks.

The vehicle

If you are driving your own car, the real cost is not zero, but it is also not the IRS mileage rate. That rate is designed for tax purposes and bakes in depreciation you would incur anyway. A practical figure for wear, tires and one oil change on a 2,000-mile trip is $120 to $200.

If you are renting, this becomes the second-largest line. A mid-size SUV for fourteen days typically runs $65 to $110 per day depending on season and location, so $910 to $1,540. Two things reduce it materially: booking an off-airport location, which avoids airport concession fees of 10 to 12 percent, and declining the counter insurance if your own policy or credit card already covers rentals. Check that before you travel rather than at the counter under pressure.

Lodging is the real variable

Look at the table again and notice that lodging swings by nearly $2,300 between the lean and comfortable columns. Nothing else moves that far. This is the lever.

Three bands are worth knowing. Campgrounds inside national parks run $20 to $35 a night and book out six months ahead. National forest and Bureau of Land Management dispersed camping is free to $15 and rarely requires a reservation, though it also has no water. Motels in gateway towns run $95 to $180 in shoulder season and $160 to $310 in peak. Anything inside a park gate is the most expensive option available and books furthest ahead.

The practical middle path most people land on is alternating: two nights camping, one night in a motel with a shower and a bed. It halves the lodging line without turning the trip into an endurance test.

Food, and the cooler

Eating three restaurant meals a day for two people over fourteen days runs $1,600 or more. Eating breakfast and lunch out of a cooler and dinner in a restaurant runs about $930. The cooler pays for itself on day two.

Budget roughly $22 per person per day for groceries and $45 per person for a mid-range sit-down dinner. Ice is $4 to $6 every other day and quietly adds $40 over two weeks. Buy a rotisserie chicken every third day. This is not sophisticated advice but it is what actually works.

The four costs almost everyone forgets

One-way rental drop fees. Flying into one city and out of another is often cheaper on airfare and then costs $150 to $400 in a drop fee. Price the round trip before assuming the open jaw is a saving.

Tolls, and the transponder problem. Several corridors in the Northeast and Texas are cashless. Rental companies charge a daily administrative fee, often $4 to $6 per day for the entire rental period, if you use any toll road at all. On a fourteen-day rental that is up to $84 for a single $2 toll. Ask about the policy at pickup, and consider routing around cashless corridors entirely.

Parking in cities on the route. A road trip that passes through Denver, Portland or Chicago for two nights adds $30 to $55 a night in garage fees. It is invisible in planning because you are thinking about parks, not parking.

The mid-trip resupply. Somewhere around day six, a real trip needs laundry, a replacement phone cable, sunscreen you forgot, an extra blanket because the desert got cold, and a pharmacy run. It is always $80 to $150 and it never appears in anyone's spreadsheet.

Build the contingency in, not around

The 10 percent contingency line in the table is not padding. Over five years of reader-reported trip costs, the single strongest predictor of a trip going over budget is having no contingency line at all, because every unexpected cost then feels like a failure rather than a plan working normally.

A blown tire is $180 to $320. A night in an unplanned motel because a road closed is $140. A stomach bug that costs you a booked tour is $90. None of these are unlikely across fourteen days. Together they are roughly the contingency line.

The short version

Fuel is about 8 percent of a road trip and gets 80 percent of the attention. Lodging is the number that decides whether your trip costs $1,700 or $8,000. Eat two meals a day out of a cooler. Check your rental company's toll policy before you touch a cashless highway. And put a real contingency line in the budget instead of pretending nothing will go wrong.
