How to stretch 15 PTO days into five real trips

Published 02/18/2026 · Updated 08/04/2026 · 11 minute read · By Rowan Delacroix

The typical American full-time worker gets between 11 and 20 paid days off in a year, and the number has barely moved in two decades. Most people spend that balance the same way: one week-long trip in the summer, a couple of long weekends, and four or five days quietly evaporating on dentist appointments, a moving day and one bad flu.

That is not a planning failure so much as an arithmetic one. Fifteen days spent as one nine-day trip plus scattered singles produces roughly one memorable trip a year. The same fifteen days, arranged deliberately around weekends and federal holidays, reliably produces four or five. The difference is not discipline. It is knowing where the free days already are.

Start by counting what you actually have

Before touching a calendar, write down three numbers. First, your PTO balance for the year, including any rollover. Second, the paid holidays your employer observes. Most US companies close for six to eleven: New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving and the day after, Christmas Day, and often Martin Luther King Jr. Day and Presidents Day. Third, be honest about how many days you need to reserve for life admin. For most people that is three to four.

So a fifteen-day balance is really about eleven or twelve travel days, sitting next to roughly nine paid holidays and 104 weekend days. Framed that way, the weekend and holiday days outnumber your PTO by ten to one. Your job is to attach PTO to them, never to spend PTO in isolation.

The two-Friday pattern

The single highest-leverage move in US trip planning is taking Friday and the following Monday off. Two PTO days produce four consecutive days away. Do that four times a year and you have spent eight days for sixteen days of travel.

Four days is genuinely enough for a real trip if the flight is under four hours. Charleston, New Orleans, Santa Fe, Portland, Montreal, Mexico City and half a dozen national parks all work on a Thursday-night-to-Monday-evening rhythm. What does not work is anything transatlantic, because you lose a full day in each direction and arrive at the point of exhaustion where the trip stops being restful.

The related move is stacking a single PTO day against an existing three-day holiday weekend. Memorial Day, Labor Day and Thanksgiving all already give you a long weekend. Adding one day on the far side turns three days into four, or two days into five. Thanksgiving is the strongest of these: taking the Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday before it costs three PTO days and produces nine consecutive days off in most companies.

Where the calendar is generous

Certain weeks in the US calendar are structurally better than others, and they repeat every year.

Notice that four of the six rows produce nine or ten days off for three or four days of PTO. That ratio is the whole game. A nine-day window is enough for Iceland, Portugal, Costa Rica, Japan if you are willing to lose two days to flying, or a proper national parks road trip.

A worked example

Here is how fifteen days can produce five trips in a single year. This is the schedule we build most often for readers who ask.

  • Trip one, late March. Two PTO days, Friday and Monday. Four days somewhere domestic and warm. Cost is low because it is nobody's peak season.
  • Trip two, Memorial Day week. Four PTO days, Tuesday through Friday, giving nine days off. This is the big one. Iceland in late May, or the Utah parks before the heat arrives.
  • Trip three, mid-August. Two PTO days around a weekend. Four days in the mountains, chosen mostly for temperature.
  • Trip four, Labor Day week. Four PTO days, giving nine days off. Europe in early September, after the August crowds and before the fall rates.
  • Trip five, Thanksgiving. Three PTO days, giving nine days off. Family for part of it, and three or four days of your own somewhere within a short flight.

That totals fifteen PTO days, thirty-five days away from work, and five distinct trips. Two of them are substantial enough to cross an ocean. Compare that to one two-week trip in July, which costs the same fifteen days, delivers one trip, and does it during the most expensive and most crowded window of the year.

Book the calendar before you book the flight

The mistake almost everyone makes is picking a destination first and then trying to find days for it. Reverse that. Open your calendar in January, mark the five windows above, and put in the PTO requests immediately. You are competing with coworkers for the same holiday-adjacent days, and the requests filed in January get approved.

Once the days are locked, choosing a destination gets easier rather than harder, because the shape of the trip is already fixed. A four-day window rules out anything over about four hours of flying. A nine-day window opens up almost everything. That constraint is useful. It is the reason our planner asks for the number of days before it asks for anything else.

Two things that quietly eat PTO

First, travel days. A trip that departs Friday at 6:00 AM and returns Monday at 11:00 PM is a four-day trip on paper and closer to two and a half days on the ground. If you can afford it, an evening departure on the Thursday costs you one hotel night and buys back most of a day. Over five trips a year that is worth more than the extra hotel nights cost.

Second, unused rollover. Many US employers cap rollover at five days or use a hard December 31 deadline. If you are carrying a balance in October, spend it. Days that expire are the most expensive days you will ever own.

The short version

Attach PTO to weekends and holidays instead of spending it alone. Prioritize the windows that give you nine days for three or four. File the requests in January. Pick the destination after the dates, not before. And treat the four-day Friday-to-Monday trip as a real trip rather than a consolation prize, because for most of the country it is the format that actually happens.

If you want to see how a specific window turns into an actual plan, open the trip planner and enter the number of days first. It will lay out one card per day, and the constraint will do most of the thinking for you.
