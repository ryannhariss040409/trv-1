First timers

Your first international trip, start to finish

Published 01/29/2026 · Updated 07/22/2026 · 13 minute read · By Nadia Okonkwo

Roughly two in five Americans hold a valid passport, which means a very large number of otherwise capable adults have never navigated a foreign immigration hall. The good news is that the process is far more boring than the internet suggests. The parts that go wrong for first-time travelers are almost never dramatic. They are paperwork deadlines, phone plans and a misunderstanding about how much cash to carry.

What follows is the sequence in the order you actually need it, from four months out to the moment you clear customs on the way home.

Four to six months out: the passport

If you do not have a passport, start here and start now. Routine processing through the US State Department has ranged from four to eleven weeks in recent years, and expedited service costs an extra fee and still takes weeks. First-time applicants must apply in person at an acceptance facility, which is usually a post office or a county clerk's office, and appointments themselves book out.

If you do have one, check the expiration date against a rule most people have never heard of. Many countries require your passport to be valid for at least three or six months beyond your planned departure date from that country. A passport that expires in four months can be refused at check-in for a trip that ends next week. Check the specific requirement on the US State Department country page for your destination.

Also check the blank pages. Some countries require two entirely blank visa pages. If your book is nearly full, renew it before the trip rather than gambling on it.

Three months out: entry requirements and vaccinations

US citizens travel visa-free to a large number of countries for tourism, but visa-free does not mean requirement-free. Several regions have added electronic pre-authorization systems in recent years: an application filed online days or weeks ahead, a small fee, and an approval tied electronically to your passport. Requirements and start dates change, so check two sources: the US State Department page for your destination, and the official government immigration website of the country itself. Do not rely on a third-party site that sells you a service you may not need.

At the same time, check the CDC travel health page for your destination. Most popular destinations need nothing beyond routine vaccinations. Some need a hepatitis A course or typhoid, and a small number in parts of Africa and South America require proof of yellow fever vaccination for entry. Vaccines take time to become effective, which is why this belongs at three months rather than three weeks.

Two to three months out: TSA PreCheck and Global Entry

These are two different programs and first-time travelers routinely confuse them.

  • TSA PreCheck speeds up security screening on departures from US airports. You keep your shoes and belt on and your laptop in the bag. It costs around $78 to $85 for five years.
  • Global Entry speeds up your re-entry into the United States at customs and immigration. It costs $120 for five years and it includes PreCheck.

If you are flying internationally at all, Global Entry is the better value because it covers both. The catch is timing: it requires an in-person interview, and interview slots at some airports book months out. Many programs now offer an enrollment-on-arrival option where you complete the interview when you land back in the US, which is worth checking. Several travel credit cards reimburse the application fee.

For a single trip taken in six weeks, skip both and arrive at the airport earlier. Neither is worth stress-applying for.

Six weeks out: money and cards

Three practical rules cover almost every destination.

Use a card with no foreign transaction fee. A typical card charges 3 percent on every purchase abroad. On a $2,500 trip that is $75 for nothing. Several no-fee cards are easy to get and the switch is worth making even for one trip.

Withdraw local currency from a bank ATM on arrival. Airport currency exchange counters offer some of the worst rates you will ever be offered. An ATM attached to an actual bank gives you the interbank rate minus your own bank's fee. When the ATM asks whether you want to be charged in US dollars, always decline. That is dynamic currency conversion and it is a markup of 3 to 7 percent.

Carry a small amount of cash and a backup card. Two hundred dollars equivalent covers taxis, tips and the one restaurant that has been cash-only since 1974. Keep the backup card somewhere separate from your wallet.

Tell your bank you are traveling if their app has that option. Card fraud systems are better than they used to be, but a declined card in a foreign supermarket at 8:00 PM is an unpleasant way to learn otherwise.

Four weeks out: your phone

You have three reasonable options and one bad one.

  • An eSIM. Buy a data plan online, install it before you fly, switch it on when you land. Typically $8 to $30 for a week or two of data. This is what most of our team does now.
  • Your carrier's international day pass. Usually $10 to $12 per day, which keeps your normal number working. Good for short trips, expensive for long ones.
  • A local SIM card bought on arrival. Cheapest per gigabyte, mildly inconvenient, and requires an unlocked phone.
  • The bad option: roaming without a plan. Pay-per-megabyte roaming charges are how people come home to a four-figure phone bill.

Whatever you choose, download offline maps for your destination before you leave, and screenshot your hotel address in the local language. Both have saved more trips than any app.

One week out: documents and the boring list

Photograph or scan your passport, your travel insurance policy if you bought one, and your accommodation confirmations. Email them to yourself so they exist somewhere that is not the phone you might lose.

Check the US State Department travel advisory for your destination. It is a four-level scale, and levels 1 and 2 cover most of the places Americans travel. Enrolling in the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program is free and lets the nearest US embassy contact you in an emergency.

On insurance: for most healthy travelers on a short trip to a country with functioning healthcare, the important coverage is trip cancellation and emergency medical evacuation, not routine medical care. Your existing health insurance almost certainly does not cover you abroad. Read what you are buying rather than clicking the box at checkout.

Departure day

Arrive three hours before an international departure. This is not padding. International check-in desks open and close on a schedule, some airlines verify your passport and onward travel manually, and the security line at a large hub at 6:00 AM is genuinely long.

Keep your passport, your cards, one change of clothes and any prescription medication in your carry-on. Checked bags occasionally take a different flight than you do.

Arrival: what actually happens

You will follow signs to immigration, join a line marked for non-citizens or all passports, and hand your passport to an officer. Most of the time you will be asked two or three questions: why you are visiting, how long you are staying, and where you are staying. Answer plainly. "Tourism, nine days, a hotel in the city center" is a complete answer. Have the hotel address available if asked.

Then you collect your bags and walk through customs, where you declare anything on the list posted at the entrance. For a normal tourist trip that is usually nothing. Green channel, walk through, done.

The whole process at a well-run airport takes twenty to forty minutes. At a busy one it can take ninety. Do not book anything time-critical for the first three hours after landing.

Coming home

Returning US citizens go through US Customs and Border Protection. You may bring back up to $800 in goods duty-free per person, and the allowance is per person, so a family of four has $3,200 between them. Declare what you bought. Undeclared purchases are the only part of this process that turns unpleasant.

Agricultural products are the common trip-up. Fresh fruit, meat, plants and seeds are restricted or prohibited, and the fines are meaningful. Sealed commercial packaged food is usually fine. If you are unsure, declare it and let the officer decide.

The short version

Passport first, and earlier than feels necessary. Check entry rules on official government sites, not on a blog. Pick one phone plan before you fly. Use a no-foreign-fee card and decline dynamic currency conversion. Arrive three hours early. Answer immigration questions plainly. Declare what you bought on the way home.

Everything else is just a trip. If you want the day-by-day part handled, our international document checklist covers the paperwork and the trip planner handles the days.
